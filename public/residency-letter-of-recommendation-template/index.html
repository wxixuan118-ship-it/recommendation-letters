Writing guide

How to use a residency letter of recommendation template

A residency letter of recommendation template helps clinical faculty write strong, effective reference letters for medical students applying to residency programs through ERAS. With thousands of applicants competing for limited residency positions each year, letters of recommendation play a critical role in helping program directors identify the strongest candidates. A well-crafted template ensures that the letter includes all the information residency programs need while saving time for busy clinicians.

The structure of a residency recommendation letter follows a predictable but important pattern. The opening paragraph should clearly state the writer’s name, title, institution, and relationship to the applicant, as well as how long they have known the student and in what capacity. It should also include a strong, clear statement of endorsement. Program directors read hundreds of letters, so they look for genuine enthusiasm backed by specific evidence rather than generic praise.

The body of the letter should provide detailed clinical examples that illustrate the applicant’s strengths in key areas. The Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education identifies six core competencies: patient care, medical knowledge, practice-based learning and improvement, interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, and systems-based practice. A strong letter addresses several of these competencies with specific clinical anecdotes rather than just listing them.

One of the most important elements of a residency recommendation letter is the comparative statement. Program directors want to know how the applicant stacks up against other medical students the writer has supervised over the years. Statements like "one of the top 5 percent of students I have worked with in my 15-year career" are far more meaningful than "a very good student." The ranking should be honest and realistic, and it should be supported by the examples provided in the letter.

Residency programs also look for evidence that the applicant has the personal qualities necessary to succeed in their specific specialty. For example, a surgical residency might look for evidence of technical skill, calm under pressure, and decisiveness. A primary care residency might focus more on communication skills, patient-centeredness, and continuity of care. The letter should ideally highlight qualities relevant to the specialty the student is pursuing, or if the writer is unsure, focus on qualities that are universally valued in residency.

Students can help their letter writers write stronger letters by providing them with useful materials. This typically includes a current CV or resume, a draft of the personal statement, a summary of clinical experiences with the writer, and a list of programs being applied to with any specific requirements. Some students also provide a "brag sheet" with specific accomplishments or moments they hope the writer will mention. However, the final letter should always be the writer’s own honest assessment.

Before submitting a residency letter of recommendation, proofread carefully for typos, grammatical errors, and factual inaccuracies. The letter should be on official institutional letterhead, signed by the writer, and submitted electronically through ERAS or the program’s application portal. The typical length is one to two pages. Letters that are too short may not provide enough information, while letters that are too long may not be read carefully by busy program directors.

When using a residency letter of recommendation template, it is important to remember that the letter represents you as a medical professional. Make sure the letter is professional, well-written, and free of errors. Ask a trusted mentor or colleague to review it before you submit it, if possible. The quality of your recommendation letters reflects on you as an applicant and on the physicians who wrote them, so taking the time to get them right is worth the effort.

Medical students should also think strategically about who they ask for recommendation letters. It is better to have a slightly less famous attending who knows you well and can write a detailed, enthusiastic letter, than a big name who can only write a generic, lukewarm one. Program directors can tell the difference, and a detailed letter from someone who worked closely with you will always be more impressive than a short form letter from a department chair you barely know.

Final review checklist

  • Opening clearly states relationship, duration, and endorsement level.
  • Clinical examples illustrate multiple ACGME competencies.
  • A comparative ranking quantifies the applicant’s standing.
  • Specialty-relevant strengths are highlighted with evidence.
  • The letter is on institutional letterhead and properly signed.

Frequently asked questions

When should I ask for residency letters of recommendation?

Ask for residency letters of recommendation early, ideally at the end of a rotation where you worked closely with the attending and performed well. This ensures the experience is fresh in their mind. Aim to have all writers lined up by early summer before you submit in September.

What makes a strong residency recommendation letter?

A strong residency recommendation letter includes specific clinical examples, a clear comparative ranking of the applicant relative to peers, evidence of key competencies, and genuine enthusiasm from a writer who knows the applicant well from a clinical setting.

Can I see my residency letters of recommendation?

Most applicants waive their right to see their letters of recommendation, as programs view waived letters as more honest and credible. However, you should ask your writers whether they feel they can write you a strong, supportive letter before you add them to your ERAS application.
